## What exactly did each idea or machine introduce, and how did it work? (chronological)

### Takeaway
From 1936 to 1975 the story runs from an imaginary machine on paper (Turing and Church, 1936) to switching logic (Shannon, 1937), then to relay, electromechanical and vacuum-tube machines (Zuse, ABC, Colossus, Mark I, ENIAC, 1938–46), then to the stored-program idea (EDVAC report 1945, Baby 1948, EDSAC 1949). After that come the transistor and the IC, which shrank the hardware (1947, 1958–59), and then the layers of software: assemblers, compilers, high-level languages, operating systems and time-sharing, structured programming, Unix and C, and Knuth's analysis of algorithms. Each step solved a concrete limitation of the step before it.

### Cited Findings

#### Floor A: 1936, Turing machine and Church's lambda calculus (Entscheidungsproblem)
- **People:** Alan Turing (1912–1954; **died 70+ years ago**) and Alonzo Church (1903–1995; not 70+) `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: Alan Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing), [Wikipedia: Alonzo Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alonzo_Church)
- **Dates:** Church submitted his paper in April 1936, a few months before Turing's paper "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem" (Proc. London Math. Soc., 1936–37). Church got to the negative answer first, using lambda calculus. Turing's paper was still published because his method was different enough — [Stanford Encyclopedia: Computability](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2006/entries/computability/); [MAA](https://old.maa.org/node/606753)
- **Key idea:** a minimal abstract machine has a finite set of states, an infinite tape of symbols, and a head that reads, writes and moves L/R according to a rule table. Turing also described a *universal* machine that reads another machine's rule table from the tape. He proved the halting problem unsolvable and got the unsolvability of the Entscheidungsproblem as a corollary. In an appendix he outlined a proof that his computability is equivalent to Church's λ-definability, which is the basis of the Church–Turing thesis — [Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2006/entries/computability/)
- **Problem solved:** it answered Hilbert's question of whether a mechanical procedure can decide every mathematical statement. The answer was no. It also gave the first precise definition of "algorithm or computation".
- **Primary text:** the Turing Archive at King's College Cambridge holds Turing's papers — [turingarchive.kings.cam.ac.uk](https://turingarchive.kings.cam.ac.uk/) `[ref, not fetched]`
- **Hands-on challenge:** (1) The player runs a given Turing machine table on a tape that holds a binary number and predicts the final tape after "increment". (2) The player writes the rule table for a unary adder (`111+11` → `11111`). (3) "Halting room": a Predict challenge with several small machines, where the player says which ones halt. The game should present this as a trap, because no general solver exists. (4) A Church side-room reduces λ-terms such as `(λx.x x)(λy.y)`, or builds Church numerals.

#### Floor B: 1937, Shannon's thesis, Boolean algebra = relay circuits
- **Person:** Claude Shannon (1916–2001; not 70+) `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: Claude Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Shannon)
- **Dates:** MIT master's thesis "A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits". The scan is dated 10 Aug 1937, and a paper drawn from it appeared in *Trans. AIEE* in 1938. It won the Alfred Noble Prize in 1940 — [MIT DSpace scan](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/11173/34541425.pdf%20?sequence=1); [summary](https://computer.fandom.com/wiki/A_Symbolic_Analysis_of_Relay_and_Switching_Circuits?oldid=8832)
- **Key idea:** switches in series are AND and switches in parallel are OR. Boolean algebra can therefore simplify the relay networks in telephone exchanges. The reverse also holds: relay networks can compute Boolean functions. The final chapter includes circuits such as a 4-bit full adder — [summary](https://computer.fandom.com/wiki/A_Symbolic_Analysis_of_Relay_and_Switching_Circuits?oldid=8832)
- **Dispute or priority:** Akira Nakashima published related switching-circuit theory in 1935–36 — [summary](https://computer.fandom.com/wiki/A_Symbolic_Analysis_of_Relay_and_Switching_Circuits?oldid=8832)
- **Problem solved:** circuit design had been done by trial and error. Shannon's algebra made it systematic, and Goldstine credited the thesis with turning digital circuit design "from an art to a science" — [summary](https://computer.fandom.com/wiki/A_Symbolic_Analysis_of_Relay_and_Switching_Circuits?oldid=8832)
- **Hands-on challenge:** the player wires relays or switches in series to make a lamp light only when A AND B are on, and in parallel for OR. Then the player builds a half adder (XOR + AND), and finally a 1-bit full adder. Bonus Fix challenge: a messy relay network that the player simplifies with Boolean laws to use fewer relays.

#### Floor C: 1938–1945, Konrad Zuse (Z1, Z3, Plankalkül)
- **Person:** Konrad Zuse (1910–1995; not 70+) — [Wikipedia: Konrad Zuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Zuse)
- **Z1** (1936–38) was a mechanical binary machine `[ref, not fetched]`. The **Z3** was completed in 1941. It used binary floating point, was relay-based and read its program from punched film. It had **no conditional branch**, and Zuse made loops by splicing the tape into rings. In 1998 Raúl Rojas showed that the Z3 is Turing-complete in principle. Zuse never thought in those terms — [Wikipedia: Z3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z3_(computer)); [Maketecheasier (secondary)](https://maketecheasier.com/konrad-zuse-z3-first-programmable-computer/)
- **Plankalkül** was designed 1943–45 and is often called the first high-level programming language design. It was not published in full until 1972, and the first compiler was built in 1998 — [Wikipedia: Konrad Zuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Zuse); [HistoryofInformation](https://historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=613)
- **Conflict:** sources disagree on when the original Z3 was destroyed in an air raid (1943 or 1944) — [Wikipedia: Z3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z3_(computer)) vs. [Maketecheasier](https://maketecheasier.com/konrad-zuse-z3-first-programmable-computer/)
- **Hands-on challenge:** the player writes a program on a strip of "punched film" that has no IF. To repeat a step, the player has to physically loop the tape. Then the player encodes a number in binary floating point (sign, exponent, mantissa).

#### Floor D: 1939–1942, Atanasoff–Berry Computer (ABC)
- **People:** John Vincent Atanasoff (1903–1995) and Clifford Berry (1918–1963). Neither died 70+ years ago `[ref, not fetched]` — [Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/john-vincent-atanasoff/)
- **Key idea:** electronic (vacuum-tube) binary arithmetic, regenerative capacitor memory on rotating drums, and a single purpose, solving systems of linear equations. CHM holds documentation of the ABC in operation — [CHM catalog 102781093](https://www.computerhistory.org/collections/catalog/102781093)
- **The court ruling:** in *Honeywell v. Sperry Rand*, decided 19 Oct 1973, Judge Earl Larson invalidated the ENIAC patent. He found that Mauchly derived key ideas from Atanasoff after visiting him and seeing the ABC in June 1941. Neither side appealed — [Wikipedia: Honeywell v. Sperry Rand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeywell,_Inc._v._Sperry_Rand_Corp.); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/news-blog/who-built-the-first-computer-2009-09-21)
- **Counterpoint:** historians point out that the ABC was not programmable, was limited to one class of problem, and was never fully functional — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/news-blog/who-built-the-first-computer-2009-09-21)
- **Hands-on challenge:** the player solves 2 linear equations by Gaussian elimination step by step, the way the ABC did, and "refreshes" fading capacitor bits on a rotating drum before they leak away.

#### Floor E: 1939–1945, Bletchley Park (Turing's Bombe, Tutte, and Flowers's Colossus)
- **People:** Tommy Flowers (1905–1998) and W. T. Tutte (1917–2002). Neither died 70+ years ago `[ref, not fetched]`. Turing worked at Bletchley from 1939 on Enigma, where the Bombe built on the Polish *bomba* `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: Alan Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing)
- **Colossus** attacked the **Lorenz (Tunny) cipher**, not Enigma. Its algorithms came from Tutte's team. Sources disagree on delivery: Nature/Copeland says January 1944 and corrects the older December 1943 date, while ETHW says delivered December 1943 and operational January 1944. It attacked its first message on 5 Feb 1944. The Mark II entered service around 1 June 1944, though other sources say May — [Nature review of Copeland](https://www.nature.com/articles/442746a); [ETHW: Colossus](https://ethw.org/Colossus); [Wikipedia: Tommy Flowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommy_Flowers)
- **How it was programmed:** with switches and patch cords. It was electronic, using vacuum tubes. ETHW says Colossus has perhaps the strongest claim to being the first programmable electronic computer, although it was special-purpose — [ETHW](https://ethw.org/Colossus)
- **Secrecy:** the machines were dismantled after the war, and Flowers was recognised publicly only after declassification in the 1970s (ETHW: 1975) — [ETHW](https://ethw.org/Colossus)
- **Hands-on challenge:** the player XORs a message with a key stream to encrypt it, then XORs again to decrypt. Then the player "counts" coincidences between two tape streams at different wheel offsets to find the setting with the highest score, which is a simplified version of Colossus's statistical method. Enigma variant: the player finds a crib position by ruling out positions where any letter maps to itself.

#### Floor F: 1944–1947, Harvard Mark I, Grace Hopper, and the moth
- **People:** Howard Aiken (1900–1973) and Grace Hopper (1906–1992). Neither died 70+ years ago `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: Grace Hopper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Hopper)
- **Mark I** (IBM ASCC) was electromechanical and became operational in 1944. Hopper joined the team that year as one of its first programmers `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: Grace Hopper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Hopper)
- **The moth (9 Sept 1947, Harvard Mark II, not Mark I):** the Smithsonian NMAH holds the logbook with the moth taped in and labelled "First actual case of bug being found". The record describes the engineers finding a moth lodged in a component. Secondary accounts give 15:45 and Relay #70, Panel F — [Smithsonian NMAH object 334663](https://www.si.edu/object/log-book-computer-bug:nmah_334663) (page not fetchable this session; summary via search); [JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/the-bug-in-the-computer-bug-story/)
- **Hands-on challenge:** "Find the moth". The Mark II gives a wrong result, and the player traces the signal relay by relay through a panel until finding the stuck relay at Panel F #70, removes the insect, and writes the log entry. A good teaching twist is that the logbook joke only works because "bug" already meant a fault.

#### Floor G: 1945–1948, ENIAC and its programmers
- **Builders:** J. Presper Eckert (1919–1995) and John Mauchly (1907–1980). **Programmers:** Jean Jennings Bartik (1924–2011), Kathleen "Kay" McNulty Mauchly Antonelli (1921–2006), Frances "Betty" Snyder Holberton (1917–2001), Marlyn Wescoff Meltzer (1922–2008), Ruth Lichterman Teitelbaum (1924–1986) and Frances Bilas Spence (1922–2012). None died 70+ years ago `[years: ref, not fetched]` — [ENIAC Programmers Project](https://eniacprogrammers.org/?p=36); [Philadelphia Citizen on Holberton](https://thephiladelphiacitizen.org/betty-holberton-eniac-six/)
- **How it worked:** ENIAC was programmed by setting switches and plugging cables between units. The six women were picked in 1945 from the human "computers" who calculated ballistics tables. They had no manuals, only logic diagrams, and before their clearances came through they were not allowed near the machine. Jennings and Snyder worked out the Master Programmer and led the preparation of the demonstration program — [Paste Magazine](https://pastemagazine.com/tech/meet-the-computers-the-women-programmers-behind-en); [eniacprogrammers.org](https://eniacprogrammers.org/?p=36)
- **Later work:** Bartik and McNulty worked on BINAC and UNIVAC I. Holberton devised BINAC's mnemonic instruction codes and contributed to the COBOL and FORTRAN standards — [Techopedia](https://www.techopedia.com/2/31652/watercooler/the-women-of-eniac-programming-pioneers)
- **Recognition:** the six were overlooked for decades and were inducted into the WITI Hall of Fame in 1997 — [Paste Magazine](https://pastemagazine.com/tech/meet-the-computers-the-women-programmers-behind-en); [Boing Boing](https://boingboing.net/2019/06/21/founding-mothers-of-computing.html)
- **Hands-on challenge:** "Plug the board". The player routes cables between an accumulator, a multiplier and the master programmer to compute a trajectory table of the form y = v·t − ½·g·t² for t = 0..10, with no keyboard. Then the player does the same calculation on the next floor as a stored program and sees how much faster it is to change.

#### Floor H: 1945–1949, von Neumann and the stored program (EDVAC report, Manchester Baby, EDSAC)
- **People:** John von Neumann (1903–1957; **reaches 70 years in Feb 2027**), Freddie Williams (1911–1977), Tom Kilburn (1921–2001) and Maurice Wilkes (1913–2010) `[years: ref, not fetched]`
- **EDVAC "First Draft"** (30 June 1945) described keeping program and data in the same memory. Only von Neumann's name appeared on it, which angered Eckert and Mauchly, whose team had developed the ideas. This is a priority dispute that the game should flag `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Draft_of_a_Report_on_the_EDVAC)
- **Turing's ACE** (proposal 1945–46) was an independent, detailed stored-program design. Pilot ACE ran in 1950 `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: Automatic Computing Engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automatic_Computing_Engine)
- **Manchester Baby (SSEM)** ran its first program on the morning of **21 June 1948**. It was a proof of concept of Williams–Kilburn tube memory with about 1,024 bits of store. The program found the highest proper factor of a number — [ETHW Milestone](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Manchester_University_%22Baby%22_Computer_and_its_Derivatives%2C_1948-1951); [Centre for Computing History](https://www.computinghistory.org.uk/det/6013/)
- **EDSAC** (Cambridge, Wilkes) ran its first programs on **6 May 1949**. It was the first full-scale stored-program machine to give a regular computing service to users. ETHW notes there are **six different "first stored-program computer" claims** in the literature — [Wikipedia: Stored-program computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stored-program_computer); [ETHW](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Manchester_University_%22Baby%22_Computer_and_its_Derivatives%2C_1948-1951)
- **Hands-on challenge:** the player gets a 32-word memory grid of bits, as on the Baby's CRT display, and enters the "highest factor" program as binary words. The player watches the instructions and data share one memory, then writes a self-modifying instruction.

#### Floor I: 1947–1965, transistor, integrated circuit, Moore's law
- **People:** John Bardeen (1908–1991), Walter Brattain (1902–1987), William Shockley (1910–1989), Jack Kilby (1923–2005), Robert Noyce (1927–1990) and Gordon Moore (1929–2023). None died 70+ years ago `[years: ref, not fetched]`
- **Transistor:** Bardeen and Brattain built the point-contact transistor at Bell Labs in December 1947 (16 Dec; demonstrated 23 Dec). Shockley developed the junction transistor in 1948, and the three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: History of the transistor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_transistor); also [Weber State reading](https://www.weber.edu/wsuimages/digitalhistory/articles/Chap4n5n6.doc)
- **IC:** Kilby (TI) demonstrated a germanium IC on **12 Sept 1958**. Noyce (Fairchild) came up with the monolithic silicon IC using the planar process in **January 1959**. The patents were fought over in court, and the two are generally credited as co-inventors — [KSU textbook](https://textbooks.cs.ksu.edu/cc110/i-concepts/08-architecture/03-integrated-circuits/); [O'Regan (Grinnell)](https://digitalage19.sites.grinnell.edu/reading/oregan-the-invention-of-the-integrated-circuit-and-the-birth-of-silicon-valley/)
- **Moore's law:** "Cramming more components onto integrated circuits", *Electronics*, 19 April 1965, predicted that component counts would double about every year. Moore revised this in 1975 to about every two years. One search result wrongly dated it to 1960 `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: Moore's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore%27s_law)
- **Problem solved:** vacuum tubes were hot and large and burned out often. Transistors are solid-state switches. ICs removed the hand-wiring of thousands of separate parts, which had been called the "tyranny of numbers".
- **Hands-on challenge:** the player replaces a rack of failing vacuum tubes with transistors in a NAND gate, builds NOT, AND and OR from NAND alone, and then "prints" the adder onto one chip. Moore's-law mini-game: the player predicts the transistor count N years later by doubling.

#### Floor J: 1947–1960, assemblers, compilers and the first languages (Hopper A-0, FORTRAN, LISP, ALGOL, COBOL)
- **Assemblers:** Kathleen Booth (1922–2022) is credited with an early assembly language (1947), and David Wheeler (1927–2004) with EDSAC's "initial orders" (1949) `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: Assembly language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_language)
- **A-0 (1952), Hopper at Remington Rand:** sources disagree on whether A-0 was a "compiler" in the modern sense or more of a linker or loader that pulled subroutines together. Hopper later led "automatic programming" from 1954 and produced MATH-MATIC and FLOW-MATIC, an English-word business language (about 1955–56) — [Wikipedia: Grace Hopper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Hopper)
- **COBOL (1959):** at the CODASYL meeting in spring 1959, the short-range committee defined COBOL, mostly from FLOW-MATIC with ideas from IBM's COMTRAN. Hopper was a technical consultant and **not the sole designer**. The game should not credit her as the "inventor of COBOL" — [Wikipedia: Grace Hopper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Hopper)
- **FORTRAN:** John Backus (1924–2007) led the IBM team that began work in 1954 and shipped FORTRAN for the IBM 704 in 1957. It was the first widely used optimizing compiler `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran)
- **LISP:** John McCarthy (1927–2011) created it in 1958, and the paper appeared in 1960 (CACM, "Recursive Functions of Symbolic Expressions…"). It introduced code-as-lists, `eval`, recursion and garbage collection `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: Lisp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisp_(programming_language))
- **ALGOL:** ALGOL 58 came first, then the ALGOL 60 report edited by Peter Naur (1928–2016), which used Backus–Naur Form. It introduced block structure and lexical scope `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: ALGOL 60](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALGOL_60)
- **Hands-on challenge:** "First compiler pass". The player writes a tokenizer that turns `A = B + C * 2` into tokens, then a translator from that to three-address assembly (`LOAD C; MUL 2; ADD B; STORE A`). LISP room: the player writes a tiny `eval` for `(+ 1 (* 2 3))`. The game ties naturally into the existing Python/JS Code challenges.

#### Floor K: 1950, Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" and the Lovelace Objection
- **Paper:** *Mind* 59 (236), 1950. It proposed the "imitation game" and answered nine objections. The Lovelace discussion is usually cited at pp. 450–451 — [SFU paper](https://journals.lib.sfu.ca/index.php/slc-uwc/article/download/7334/6190/28197); [The Morning Paper summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2017/10/20/computing-machinery-and-intelligence/)
- **Objection:** Lovelace (1843) wrote that the Analytical Engine "has no pretensions to originate anything". Turing restated it as "a machine can never do anything really new" or "never take us by surprise". He replied that machines surprise him frequently, because predicting what a program will do is hard. He also asked whether humans themselves originate anything that does not grow from earlier teaching, and he proposed "learning machines" that are educated like a child — [The Morning Paper](https://blog.acolyer.org/2017/10/20/computing-machinery-and-intelligence/); [SFU paper](https://journals.lib.sfu.ca/index.php/slc-uwc/article/download/7334/6190/28197)
- **Tie-in:** this connects directly with the Lovelace Floor (Floor 2). The game can stage a debate where the player argues Turing's side.
- **Hands-on challenge:** Predict challenges where a tiny program, such as a cellular automaton or the Collatz loop, produces output the player fails to guess. That is "being surprised by a machine". Also an imitation-game round in which the player judges two text replies.

#### Floor L: 1948, Shannon's information theory
- "A Mathematical Theory of Communication", *Bell System Technical Journal*, July and October 1948. It introduced entropy H = −Σ p log₂ p, channel capacity and the bit as the unit (Shannon credited the word "bit" to John Tukey) `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: A Mathematical Theory of Communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Mathematical_Theory_of_Communication)
- **Hands-on challenge:** the player computes the entropy of a message, builds a prefix code that beats fixed-width encoding, and adds a parity bit to detect a flipped bit on a noisy channel.

#### Floor M: 1961–1969, operating systems and time-sharing (CTSS, Multics)
- **CTSS:** Fernando Corbató (1926–2019) and colleagues at MIT demonstrated it in November 1961 on an IBM 709. Users at terminals shared one CPU through time slices. **Multics** (1965–, MIT + GE + Bell Labs) introduced a hierarchical file system, rings of protection and other features. Bell Labs left in 1969 `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: CTSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compatible_Time-Sharing_System); [Wikipedia: Multics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multics)
- **Problem solved:** in batch processing a user submitted cards and waited hours. Time-sharing gave each user the illusion of a whole machine.
- **Hands-on challenge:** the player schedules 4 jobs on one CPU with round-robin (quantum = 2), compares average waiting time with FIFO, and handles a job that blocks on I/O.

#### Floor N: 1961–1969, Margaret Hamilton and the Apollo Guidance Computer
- **Person:** Margaret Hamilton (b. 1936). **Her status needs checking:** a search result titled "Margaret Hamilton, RIP" appeared ([mjtsai.com](https://mjtsai.com/blog/?p=53476)), but I could not confirm whether it refers to her. Either way she did not die 70+ years ago.
- **1202/1201 alarms (20 July 1969):** during the lunar descent the AGC raised five program alarms in about four minutes. 1202 means executive overflow, too many jobs for the time available. A radar switch setting fed the computer unneeded data. The priority-driven executive, designed by Hal Laning and developed by Hamilton's team, dropped low-priority jobs and restarted the high-priority guidance work. Steve Bales gave "Go". **Source caveat:** the only results I found were blog-grade (siliconcanals, scienceblog, huizhou92), so these details should be checked against NASA or MIT sources or the AGC source code — [huizhou92](https://pub.huizhou92.com/the-1202-alarm-how-64-kilobytes-and-an-asynchronous-loop-saved-apollo-11-b6c354e47c25); [scienceblog](https://scienceblog.com/?p=582954)
- **Hands-on challenge:** the player builds a priority scheduler with a fixed CPU budget per tick. When the "radar" job floods the queue, the scheduler must shed low-priority jobs and keep "guidance" and "throttle" running. If it does not, the lander crashes.

#### Floor O: 1956–1972, Dijkstra (algorithms and structured programming)
- **Person:** Edsger W. Dijkstra (1930–2002). He designed his shortest-path algorithm in 1956 and published it in 1959. He introduced semaphores (around 1965) and the THE multiprogramming system. His letter "Go To Statement Considered Harmful" appeared in CACM in March 1968, with the title chosen by the editor Niklaus Wirth. He received the Turing Award in 1972 `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: Edsger W. Dijkstra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edsger_W._Dijkstra)
- **Hands-on challenge:** the player finds the shortest route between Tower floors with Dijkstra's algorithm, rewrites a GOTO-spaghetti program as loops and ifs (a Fix challenge), and uses a semaphore to stop two processes from both grabbing one printer.

#### Floor P: 1969–1974, UNIX and C (Thompson, Ritchie)
- **People:** Ken Thompson (b. 1943; living) and Dennis Ritchie (1941–2011).
- **Timeline:** in 1969 Thompson wrote Unix in assembler on a PDP-7 and then wrote the language B. On the PDP-11 Ritchie added types to B ("NB"), and that became C between 1969 and 1973. In 1973 Unix, kernel included, was rewritten in C, so it could be ported to other machines — [Ritchie, "The Development of the C Language" (Bell Labs)](https://www.nokia.com/bell-labs/about/dennis-m-ritchie/chist.html); [Bell Labs: B to C](https://bell-labs.com/unix-history/btoc.html); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com:443/print/article/1532873)
- **Hands-on challenge:** the player chains small tools with pipes (`grep | sort | uniq -c`) to answer a question, and ports a function from "B" (untyped words) to "C" by adding types and fixing a byte-versus-word bug.

#### Floor Q: 1962–1975, Donald Knuth
- **Person:** Donald Knuth (b. 1938; living). *The Art of Computer Programming* Vol. 1 (1968), Vol. 2 (1969) and Vol. 3 (1973) put the analysis of algorithms on a rigorous footing, and he received the Turing Award in 1974 `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: Donald Knuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Knuth)
- **Hands-on challenge:** the player counts the comparisons that insertion sort and merge sort make on the same input, predicts the counts for n=1000, and receives a "Knuth reward check" for spotting a planted error in the floor's text.

### Inferences
- The floors divide naturally into theory (Turing, Church, Shannon), hardware (Zuse to IC), the stored-program jump (EDVAC, Baby, EDSAC), the language layer (assembler to COBOL, FORTRAN, LISP, ALGOL), the systems layer (time-sharing, Apollo, Unix) and the craft of programming (Dijkstra, Knuth). Boss Floors could sit at the transitions: Colossus or ENIAC, then the stored program, then Apollo.
- Several challenges reuse earlier ones. The half adder from Floor B becomes the IC on Floor I, and the ENIAC trajectory on Floor G becomes a stored program on Floor H. That helps show progress across floors.
- Turing is the only major figure in this era who died more than 70 years ago. Every other floor would probably need period-generic characters, such as "a relay engineer" or "an ENIAC programmer", or a legal review, unless the design team decides otherwise.

## Common myths to avoid

### Takeaway
The most common errors are calling the moth the origin of "bug", crediting a single "first computer", crediting von Neumann alone for the stored-program idea or Hopper alone for COBOL, and repeating the claim that the ENIAC women were simply "operators".

### Cited Findings
- **The moth did not coin "bug".** The logbook says "first *actual* case", which was a joke that only works because "bug" already meant a fault. Edison used "bug" for electrical faults in the 1870s. Fred Shapiro calls the story "not very etymologically correct" — [JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/the-bug-in-the-computer-bug-story/)
- **Hopper probably did not find or tape the moth herself.** The logbook does not record who removed it. It was her team's machine, the **Mark II**, and she told the story often — [JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/the-bug-in-the-computer-bug-story/); [Smithsonian NMAH](https://www.si.edu/object/log-book-computer-bug:nmah_334663)
- **There is no single "first computer".** The ABC was the first electronic digital computing device in the 1973 court ruling, but it was not programmable. The Z3 was the first working programmable machine (relay, 1941), but it had no conditional branch. Colossus was the first programmable electronic machine, but it was special-purpose and kept secret until the 1970s. ENIAC was the first general-purpose electronic machine, but it was programmed by plugging cables. The Baby was the first stored program (1948), and EDSAC the first stored-program computer in regular service (1949). ETHW alone lists six "first stored-program" claims — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/news-blog/who-built-the-first-computer-2009-09-21); [ETHW: Colossus](https://ethw.org/Colossus); [ETHW Baby Milestone](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Manchester_University_%22Baby%22_Computer_and_its_Derivatives%2C_1948-1951); [Wikipedia: Z3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z3_(computer))
- **The 1973 ruling is a legal finding, not a consensus among historians** — [Wikipedia: Honeywell v. Sperry Rand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeywell,_Inc._v._Sperry_Rand_Corp.)
- **ENIAC programmers:** the six women wrote the programs and debugged the machine with almost no documentation. They were not mere "operators", and they were largely left out of the 1946 publicity — [eniacprogrammers.org](https://eniacprogrammers.org/?p=36); [Boing Boing](https://boingboing.net/2019/06/21/founding-mothers-of-computing.html)
- **COBOL was a committee design** (CODASYL 1959) based on FLOW-MATIC and COMTRAN. Hopper was a consultant and its main champion, not the sole inventor — [Wikipedia: Grace Hopper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Hopper)
- **A-0 as "first compiler" is disputed.** Some sources call it a linker or loader — [Wikipedia: Grace Hopper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Hopper)
- **Church got there first.** The undecidability of the Entscheidungsproblem was published by Church slightly before Turing — [MAA](https://old.maa.org/node/606753)
- **Colossus broke Lorenz, not Enigma.** The Turing–Welchman Bombe was used against Enigma — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/442746a); [ETHW](https://ethw.org/Colossus)
- **Shannon was not the only one:** Nakashima's switching theory ran in parallel (1935–36) — [summary](https://computer.fandom.com/wiki/A_Symbolic_Analysis_of_Relay_and_Switching_Circuits?oldid=8832)
- **The IC had two inventors**, Kilby and Noyce — [KSU textbook](https://textbooks.cs.ksu.edu/cc110/i-concepts/08-architecture/03-integrated-circuits/)
- **Moore's law** is an empirical observation and prediction (1965, revised 1975), not a physical law `[ref, not fetched]` — [Wikipedia: Moore's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore%27s_law)

### Inferences
- The "find the moth" floor works well as a gag if the Guide line explicitly corrects the myth, for example: "They wrote *actual* because engineers already said bug."
- "First computer" makes good Quiz material if the questions require a qualifier, such as first programmable, first electronic or first stored-program.

## Accurate ordering and overlaps

### Takeaway
Many of these events overlapped and happened independently: wartime secrecy meant Zuse, Bletchley, Iowa and the Moore School were working in parallel without knowledge of each other. A strictly linear Tower should treat the 1937–1949 floors as parallel threads, not as a chain.
