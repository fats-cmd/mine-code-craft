## What exactly did each device or idea do, how did it work, and what floor challenge fits it? (chronological entries)

### Takeaway
The pre-1936 story has two threads. One is **mechanising arithmetic**: abacus → logarithms/bones/slide rule → Schickard/Pascal/Leibniz calculators → Babbage's engines → Hollerith's tabulators. The other is **formalising reasoning**: Leibniz's binary → Boole/De Morgan → Frege → Hilbert → Gödel → Church/Turing 1936. The two threads meet in Lovelace's Notes (1843) and in Turing's 1936 paper. Each step below has a concrete mechanism that a player could act out.

### Cited Findings

**1. Counting and the abacus (prehistory to c. 300 BC, and onward)**
- Ishango bone: found in 1950 in the DR Congo, with notches in three columns, dated to about 20,000 years ago. Its purpose is debated: some read it as a tally, others as a lunar calendar. — [KASS](https://kartsci.org/kocomu/computer-history/history-abacus-ancient-computing/); [notAcalculator](https://notacalculator.com/guides/history-of-calculation) (both are weak, popular sources; treat the dating and purpose as disputed)
- Salamis Tablet: the oldest surviving counting board. It is marble, about 150 × 75 × 4.5 cm, dated to about 300 BC and found on Salamis in 1846. Its origin is disputed: one source says Babylonian, another says most likely Greek. — [Wikipedia: Salamis Tablet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salamis_Tablet); [TMU abacus history](https://www.ee.torontomu.ca/~elf/abacus/history.html)
- One paper argues the Salamis Tablet was probably the template for the Roman hand abacus. This is an inference from layout, not documented descent. — [arXiv 1206.4349 "Ancient Computers"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1206.4349)
- Chinese suanpan: dating conflicts. One source says about 1200 AD, another ties its major development to the Ming dynasty (1368–1644). — [abacusmpe](https://abacusmpe.wordpress.com/2020/07/13/history-of-abacus/); [FasterCapital](https://fastercapital.com/content/History--Tracing-the-Historical-Origins-of-the-Abacus.html) (both low quality, so the date is a gap)
- *How it works:* place value is held by position (column or line). Counters or beads stand for units, and on a suanpan "heaven" beads are worth 5. A carry means clearing a column and adding one counter to the next column. (The mechanism is general knowledge, consistent with the sources above.)
- *Problem solved:* adding and subtracting large numbers (trade, taxes) without written place-value numerals.
- *People:* anonymous, so there is no issue with depicting real people.
- **Challenge:** "Add 478 + 365 on a counting board": the player moves counters and must do the carries by hand. A harder version: a suanpan with 5-beads.

**2. Antikythera mechanism (built c. 2nd century BC; found 1901)**
- Freeth et al., "Decoding the ancient Greek astronomical calculator known as the Antikythera Mechanism", *Nature* 444, 587–591 (2006), DOI 10.1038/nature05357. The team used surface imaging and high-resolution X-ray tomography, rebuilt the gear functions, doubled the number of deciphered inscriptions, and showed that it predicted lunar and solar eclipses using Babylonian arithmetic-progression cycles. The device was found in 1901 in a Roman-era shipwreck and dates to about the late 2nd century BC. — [Cardiff ORCA record](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/1622/); [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/decoding-antikythera-mechanism-first-computer-180953979/)
- **Disputed dating:** in 2014, Carman & Evans and, separately, Freeth placed the eclipse-predictor epoch in **205–187 BCE**, with a base date near 12 May 205 BCE. This is the epoch of the dials, **not** necessarily when the device was built. Edmunds, Iversen, Jones and Wright favour a build date much closer to the shipwreck. Carman (2017) estimated the "final date" at about 193 BCE. — [Evans & Carman, Archive for History of Exact Sciences 68(6):693–774 (2014), PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/EVAOTE-2); [Carman 2017, JHA](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0021828617721553); [Jones 2020, ISAW Papers 17](http://dlib.nyu.edu/awdl/isaw/isaw-papers/17/); [Phys.org 2014](https://phys.org/news/2014-11-antikythera-mechanism-clues-ancient-greek.html)
- *How it works:* a hand crank drives a train of bronze gears. Gear ratios encode astronomical cycles, and dials show the positions of the Sun and Moon, the calendar, and eclipse predictions on a back spiral dial (the Saros). It is an **analogue** computer: it models ratios, not digits. — [Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/1622/); [CACM "The Antikythera Mechanism"](https://cacm.acm.org/research/the-antikythera-mechanism/)
- I did not verify the exact gear count, the Metonic (19-year) and Callippic dials, or the pin-and-slot lunar anomaly mechanism in a source this session **[UNVERIFIED; check the Nature paper]**.
- *People:* the maker is unknown. Linking it to Hipparchus or Archimedes is speculation; do not present it as fact.
- **Challenge:** "Turn the crank to predict the next eclipse": choose gear tooth counts so that the ratio matches a cycle (for example, turn a 19-year dial so that months and years line up), then read the Saros dial.

**3. Napier: logarithms (1614) and "bones" (1617)**
- Napier published his logarithm tables, *Mirifici logarithmorum canonis descriptio*, in Edinburgh in 1614. — [Thomas Fisher Rare Book Library](https://fisherdigitus.library.utoronto.ca/exhibits/show/sciencehighlights/napier); [IEEE Computer Pioneers](https://history.computer.org/pioneers/napier.html)
- In 1617 *Rabdologiae* described simple calculating devices, including the rods called "Napier's bones". — [National Museums Scotland](https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/napiers-bones-the-logarithmic-genius-who-revolutionised-calculations); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/science-and-technology/mathematics-biographies/john-napier)
- *How it works:* **logarithms** turn multiplication into addition (log ab = log a + log b): you look up two logs, add them, and look up the antilog. **Bones** are rods printed with the multiplication table of one digit, split by diagonals. Lay the rods side by side and add along the diagonals to read off a multi-digit product (lattice multiplication). (The mechanism is general knowledge.)
- *Person:* John Napier, 1550–1617 **[UNVERIFIED years]**. Died more than 70 years ago.
- **Challenge:** "Multiply 46 × 7 with Napier's bones": choose rods 4 and 6, read row 7, and add the diagonals with a carry. A second challenge: "multiply with a log table" by adding two looked-up values.

**4. Slide rule (Gunter's scale, c. 1620s; Oughtred, invented c. 1622, printed 1632/1633)**
- Edmund Gunter's logarithmic scale (a single ruler used with dividers): one source dates it to 1624. I found no source for the common date of "1620". — [Wikipedia: History of computing hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_computing_hardware)
- William Oughtred's **circular** slide rule was described in print in 1632 and his **straight** rule in 1633. His invention is said to date from about 8 years before Wingate's 1630 publication, which gives c. 1622. — [Cajori, *On the History of Gunter's Scale and the Slide Rule* (Gutenberg)](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/42216/42216-h/42216-h.htm)
- **Priority dispute:** Richard Delamain, Oughtred's former pupil, claimed the circular rule. Cajori and the Oughtred Society favour Oughtred. — [Cajori (Gutenberg)](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/42216/42216-h/42216-h.htm); [thonyc blog (weaker source)](https://thonyc.wordpress.com/2026/08/13/oughtred-vs-delamain-a-dispute-over-teaching-methods-and-who-invented-the-circular-slide-rule/)
- *How it works:* two logarithmic scales slide against each other. Sliding them adds lengths, which multiplies numbers. You keep track of the decimal point yourself.
- *People:* Gunter 1581–1626 and Oughtred 1574–1660 **[UNVERIFIED years]**. Both died more than 70 years ago.
- **Challenge:** "Slide C against D to multiply 2.5 × 3.2" and place the decimal point yourself. Players also learn that slide rules give about 3 significant figures, not exact answers.

**5. Schickard's "calculating clock" (1623–1624)**
- Schickard described his machine in letters to Kepler in 1623 and 1624. It is usually called the earliest documented mechanical calculator. It added and subtracted six-digit numbers and rang a bell on overflow, and it was meant to ease Kepler's astronomical calculations. — [Wikipedia: Wilhelm Schickard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Schickard); [howmachinesthink substack](https://howmachinesthink.substack.com/p/issue-16-the-first-mechanical-calculators)
- **Disputed:** whether it was finished, and what happened to it. One source says a copy burned in a fire. The design was rediscovered in the 20th century among Kepler's papers. Reconstructions are modern. — [peterschulte.org (weak source)](https://peterschulte.org/good-news/wilhelm-schickard-calculating-clock-mechanical-calculator/); [Wikipedia: Wilhelm Schickard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Schickard)
- *How it works (general knowledge, check before use):* the upper part held Napier's bones on cylinders for multiplication. The lower part was an adding mechanism with single-tooth carry wheels.
- *People:* Wilhelm Schickard 1592–1635 and Johannes Kepler 1571–1630 **[UNVERIFIED years]**. Both died more than 70 years ago.
- **Challenge:** "Overflow!": add numbers until the six-digit register overflows and the bell rings, which teaches fixed-width registers and overflow.

**6. Pascal's Pascaline (1642)**
- Pascal's machine dates from 1642. It improved on earlier carry handling by separating the carry from the input stroke and using gravity: a weighted lever called the *sautoir*. About 50 were made, and it was built to help with tax calculations (Pascal's father was a tax official). — [howmachinesthink substack](https://howmachinesthink.substack.com/p/issue-16-the-first-mechanical-calculators); [Wikipedia: Stepped reckoner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stepped_reckoner)
- *How it works:* each digit wheel lifts a weighted *sautoir* as it turns from 0 toward 9. When it passes 9→0 the weight drops and pushes the next wheel on by one. This lets a carry ripple across many digits without jamming. Subtraction is done with nines' complements (general knowledge **[UNVERIFIED detail]**).
- *Person:* Blaise Pascal 1623–1662 **[UNVERIFIED years]**. Died more than 70 years ago.
- **Challenge:** "Set the Pascaline's carry": enter 999 + 1 and watch the carry ripple. A puzzle variant: subtract using nines' complement on an add-only machine.

**7. Leibniz: stepped reckoner (1672–1673) and binary (1703)**
- Leibniz got the idea in Paris in 1672 and showed a wooden model to the Royal Society in London on 1 February 1673. Sources disagree on whether the machine dates from 1672 or 1673. — [Wikipedia: Stepped reckoner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stepped_reckoner); [howmachinesthink substack](https://howmachinesthink.substack.com/p/issue-16-the-first-mechanical-calculators)
- *How it works:* the **stepped drum** (Leibniz wheel) is a cylinder with nine teeth of increasing length. Sliding a small gear along it chooses how many teeth engage (0–9) on each turn. This allows multiplication by repeated addition plus shifting the carriage. Only two machines were built. — [Wikipedia: Stepped reckoner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stepped_reckoner); [howmachinesthink](https://howmachinesthink.substack.com/p/issue-16-the-first-mechanical-calculators)
- **Binary:** Leibniz's "Explication de l'arithmétique binaire" appeared in the Paris Académie royale des sciences publication in 1703 (one source gives 5 May 1703). It was his only publication on binary arithmetic. It links binary to the I Ching hexagrams (the Fuxi/Xiantiantu ordering), which he received from the Jesuit Joachim Bouvet in a letter that reached him on 1 April 1703. — [Journal of East-West Thought (CSU ScholarWorks)](https://scholarworks.calstate.edu/downloads/xk81jr681); [Bauman Rare Books listing](https://www.baumanrarebooks.com/rare-books/leibnitz-g-w-von-bernoulli-jakob/explication-de-l-arithmetique-binaire/75458.aspx)
- **Disputed:** whether the I Ching influenced Leibniz's binary, or whether he had already worked it out and the hexagrams only shaped how he presented it. — [Journal of East-West Thought](https://scholarworks.calstate.edu/downloads/xk81jr681); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361177486_THE_DEVELOPMENT_OF_BINARY_ARITHMETIC_BY_LEIBNIZ_INFLUENCE_OR_INDEPENDENCE_REGARDING_THE_XIANTIANTU_OF_SHAO_YONG)
- *Person:* Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz 1646–1716 **[UNVERIFIED years]**. Died more than 70 years ago.
- **Challenges:** "Multiply 237 × 4 by cranking": turn the crank 4 times, then shift the carriage. "Write your age in binary". "Decode a hexagram as a 6-bit number".

**8. Jacquard loom (precursors 1725–1745; Jacquard's machine c. 1804)**
- Bouchon (1725) controlled a loom with perforated paper tape. Falcon (1728; some sources say 1725) switched to a chain of punched cards. Vaucanson (1745) tried to automate it. — [Wikipedia: Jacquard machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacquard_machine); [historyofpunchcards](https://historyofpunchcards.wordpress.com/category/1-emergence-of-punchcards-the-jacquard-loom/)
- Jacquard's card-controlled loom is usually dated 1804, with one source putting it in working order by April 1805. Jacquard patented looms in 1800–1801. One source says he never patented the punched-card device itself, which combined earlier cards with Vaucanson's mechanism. The cards were laced into a continuous chain. — [Wikipedia: Joseph Marie Jacquard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Marie_Jacquard); [Wikipedia: Jacquard machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacquard_machine)
- A woven silk portrait of Jacquard needed about 24,000 cards. Each hole decided whether a warp thread was lifted, so it set which thread showed at each point. — [Wikipedia: Joseph Marie Jacquard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Marie_Jacquard); [CHM blog: Preserving the Legend of J. M. Jacquard](https://computerhistory.org/blog/artifact-adopted-preserving-the-legend-of-j-m-jacquard/)
- Babbage took the punched-card idea from the Jacquard loom for the Analytical Engine. — [Raspberry Pi: Analytical Engine](https://www.raspberrypi.com/news/charles-babbages-analytical-engine-the-computers-that-made-the-world/)
- *Person:* Joseph-Marie Jacquard 1752–1834 **[UNVERIFIED years]**. Died more than 70 years ago.
- **Challenge:** "Weave a pattern by punching cards": each card is one row of the pattern, where a hole lifts that thread and shows colour A. The player punches a short chain of cards that weaves a target sprite. This is a good lead-in to bitmaps and to "a program is data on cards".

**9. Charles Babbage: Difference Engine No. 1 (1820s–1833), Analytical Engine (from 1834), Difference Engine No. 2 (designed 1847–49; built 1991/2002)**
- Difference Engine No. 1 was built by engineer Joseph Clement. A "finished portion of the unfinished engine" was completed in 1832. It had about 2,000 parts, one-seventh of the full design, and it worked. — [Science Museum](https://www.sciencemuseum.org.uk/objects-and-stories/charles-babbages-difference-engines-and-science-museum)
- The full 1830 design needed about 25,000 parts and would have weighed about 4 tons. — [Wikipedia: Difference engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_engine)
- Construction stopped in 1833 when Clement downed tools after a dispute over compensation for moving his workshop. — [CHM: A Brief History](https://www.computerhistory.org/babbage/history/). Some sources say 1832. The government formally ended funding in 1842 (Peel, on Goulburn's advice). It had spent about £17,000, or £15,288 1s 4d not counting buildings, depending on the source. — [Wikipedia: Difference engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_engine); [thonyc blog on Airy and financing](https://thonyc.wordpress.com/2019/12/26/christmas-trilogy-2019-part-2-babbage-airy-and-financing-the-difference-engine/)
- *How the Difference Engine works:* the **method of finite differences**. For a polynomial of degree n, the n-th differences are constant. So once the first value and its differences are set up, every next table value comes from **additions only**: add the 2nd difference to the 1st, then the 1st to the value, and repeat. The machine did this with columns of geared digit wheels. Its purpose was to remove human copying and calculating errors from printed mathematical tables. — [CHM: The Engines](https://www.computerhistory.org/babbage/engines/); [babbagedifferenceengine site](https://sites.google.com/site/babbagedifferenceengine/howitwasintendedtowork)
- In 1834, with the Difference Engine stalled, Babbage conceived the **Analytical Engine**. — [CHM: A Brief History](https://www.computerhistory.org/babbage/history/)
- *How the Analytical Engine works:* the **Store** (memory, planned for about 1,000 numbers of 50 digits) and the **Mill** (arithmetic unit) were separate. It was programmed with Jacquard-style punched cards (operation cards and variable cards) and allowed conditional branching and looping. It is general-purpose in design. — [Raspberry Pi](https://www.raspberrypi.com/news/charles-babbages-analytical-engine-the-computers-that-made-the-world/); [Stanford CS project page](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/eroberts/courses/soco/projects/1998-99/babbage/ana-mech.htm)
- **How much was built:** only a small trial piece of the Analytical Engine was finished before Babbage died in 1871. His son Henry Babbage built part of the mill later (one source says 1889). — [Science Museum](https://www.sciencemuseum.org.uk/objects-and-stories/charles-babbages-difference-engines-and-science-museum); [ScienceDirect topic overview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/analytical-engine)
- **Difference Engine No. 2** was designed in 1847–1849 **[dates UNVERIFIED this session; CHM gives them]**. The Science Museum built the calculating section to Babbage's drawings: the main part was finished in June 1991 and the calculating section completed November 1991, for his bicentenary, under curator Doron Swade. The printer and stereotyper were added in 2002, paid for by Nathan Myhrvold. The machine has about 4,000 parts and the calculating section weighs about 2.6 tons (sources vary: 2.6 to 3 tons, and some say 5 tons with the printer). A second complete copy went on display at CHM, Mountain View, from 10 May 2008. — [Science Museum Group collection co62748](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co62748); [CHM: A Modern Sequel](https://www.computerhistory.org/babbage/modernsequel/); [historyofinformation.com](https://historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=1138)
- *People:* Charles Babbage 1791–1871 (death year confirmed by [Science Museum](https://www.sciencemuseum.org.uk/objects-and-stories/charles-babbages-difference-engines-and-science-museum); birth year 1791 is implied by the 1991 bicentenary, [Science Museum Group](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co62748)). Joseph Clement 1779–1844 **[UNVERIFIED]**. Both died more than 70 years ago. **Doron Swade and Nathan Myhrvold are living**, so do not depict them as characters.
- **Challenges:** "Compute a table of squares (or of x² + x + 41) by finite differences": the player sets the initial value, 1st difference and 2nd difference columns, then cranks. Each crank is just two additions, and the player checks the table. "Find the bug in the printed table", which is why the engine existed. For the Analytical Engine: "write a card deck" that loops (operation cards + variable cards, Store ↔ Mill).

**10. Luigi Menabrea and Ada Lovelace's Notes (1842–1843)**
- Menabrea's paper on the Analytical Engine came out of Babbage's 1840 talks in Turin. It was published in French in 1842 **[journal (Bibliothèque universelle de Genève) UNVERIFIED this session]**. Charles Wheatstone suggested that Lovelace translate it for *Taylor's Scientific Memoirs*, and Babbage suggested she add notes. — [howmachinesthink substack](https://howmachinesthink.substack.com/p/issue-18-ada-lovelace-the-first-programmer); [Wikipedia: Scientific Memoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Memoirs)
- It was published in August 1843 and signed only "A.A.L.". Her Notes **A to G** take up about 41 of the 66 pages, roughly three times the length of Menabrea's text. — [Wikipedia: Note G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_G); [howmachinesthink](https://howmachinesthink.substack.com/p/issue-18-ada-lovelace-the-first-programmer)
- **Note G** is the last of the seven notes. It sets out, step by step and as a table, how the Engine would compute the **Bernoulli numbers**. It was never run because the Engine was never built. — [Wikipedia: Note G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_G); [Bodleian blog](https://blogs.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/adalovelace/2018/07/26/ada-lovelace-and-the-analytical-engine/)
- **Authorship (disputed):** in his 1864 memoir *Passages from the Life of a Philosopher*, Babbage wrote that he suggested the Bernoulli example, credited her with "the algebraical working" and most of the rest, and said she found "a grave mistake" he had made. Revisionists use this to argue that the program was mainly Babbage's. More recent work stresses collaboration: summer-1843 letters show she did not just copy, and Misa (2019) cites documentary evidence that Lovelace herself wrote out the Note G table in pencil. — [Wikipedia: Note G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_G); [CACM: "Was Ada Lovelace Actually the First Programmer?"](https://cacm.acm.org/blogcacm/was-ada-lovelace-actually-the-first-programmer/); [ResearchGate: Babbage, Lovelace and the Bernoulli Numbers](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366984308_Charles_Babbage_Ada_Lovelace_and_the_Bernoulli_Numbers); [Oxford: The mathematics of Ada Lovelace](https://www.maths.ox.ac.uk/groups/history-mathematics/mathematics-ada-lovelace)
- **Her mathematical training:** Augustus De Morgan tutored her by post (a "correspondence course") for about 18 months in 1840–1841, ending in late 1841 or early 1842. — [Hollings, Martin & Rice, "The Lovelace–De Morgan mathematical correspondence", Historia Mathematica (2017)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0315086017300319); [Clay Mathematics Institute: Lovelace's papers](https://www.claymath.org/online-resources/ada-lovelaces-mathematical-papers/)
- **"Lovelace's Objection":** in "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (*Mind*, 1950), section 6 is "Lady Lovelace's Objection". Turing quotes her: "The Analytical Engine has no pretensions to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform." Turing rejects it as decisive. He took the quote by way of Hartree (1949). — [Turing 1950, Mind (Oxford e-library PDF)](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/activities/ieg/e-library/sources/t_article.pdf); [SEP: The Turing Test](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/turing-test/)
- *How it works / key idea:* the Engine handles **symbols, not only numbers**. A program is a series of operations on variables, with loops ("cycles"). Turing's paper attributes the memoir to 1842, which is a date confusion: the translation was published in 1843.
- *People:* Ada Lovelace 1815–1852, Luigi Federico Menabrea 1809–1896, Charles Wheatstone 1802–1875, Augustus De Morgan 1806–1871 **[UNVERIFIED years]**. All died more than 70 years ago.
- **Challenges:** "Trace Note G": run a simplified version of her table (variables V1…Vn, one operation per row) and find the **known transposition bug** in the published table (see Gaps; verify which row first). "Answer Lovelace's Objection": a Quiz floor where the player argues for or against "a machine can only do what we order it to".

**11. Boole, De Morgan and the algebra of logic (1847, 1854)**
- Boole's *Mathematical Analysis of Logic* (1847) is the usual start of modern logic. *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought* (1854) is the larger, more complete work, giving algorithms for analysing argument forms. — [SEP: George Boole](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/boole/); [SEP: Algebra of Logic Tradition](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/algebra-logic-tradition/)
- De Morgan's *Formal Logic* also came out in 1847, reportedly on almost the same day as Boole's book. De Morgan was among the first to treat the logic of relations symbolically. — [SEP: Algebra of Logic Tradition](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/algebra-logic-tradition/); [ericgerlach.com](https://ericgerlach.com/logic-mill-de-morgan-boole/)
- *How it works:* classes or propositions become algebraic variables, with 1 = universe and 0 = nothing. Logical "and" behaves like multiplication, and x·x = x (idempotence). De Morgan's laws: ¬(A∧B) = ¬A∨¬B and ¬(A∨B) = ¬A∧¬B. (Standard content.) Boole's own algebra used exclusive-style addition and is **not identical** to modern Boolean algebra, which was tidied up later by Jevons, Peirce and others **[UNVERIFIED nuance; see SEP Boole]**.
- *People:* George Boole 1815–1864, De Morgan 1806–1871 **[UNVERIFIED years]**. Both died more than 70 years ago.
- **Challenge:** "Simplify a Boolean expression": cut ¬(¬A ∨ ¬B) ∨ (A ∧ C) down to the fewest gates using De Morgan's laws and x·x = x, then check with a truth table.

**12. Gottlob Frege, *Begriffsschrift* (1879)**
- *Begriffsschrift* (1879) was developed entirely apart from the Boole/De Morgan tradition and had little influence at first. Frege's work dates from 1879–1903. — [SEP: Emergence of First-Order Logic](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/logic-firstorder-emergence/); [Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://www.rep.routledge.com/articles/thematic/logic-in-the-19th-century/v-1/sections/logic-and-mathematics-frege)
- *Key idea:* a formal language with quantifiers ("for all", "there exists") and variables, and rigorous rules of inference. This is the basis of predicate logic, which made it possible to ask whether proof itself could be mechanised.
- *Person:* Gottlob Frege 1848–1925 **[UNVERIFIED years]**. Died more than 70 years ago. (Caution: his later private diaries contain antisemitic views. A depiction should neither glorify nor ignore this. **[UNVERIFIED this session]**)
- **Challenge:** "Translate a sentence into a formula": "every climber has a key" → ∀x(Climber(x) → ∃k HasKey(x,k)), then find a counter-model.

**13. Herman Hollerith and the 1890 census (1880s–1924)**
- Hollerith built electric tabulators that read and sorted punched cards for the 1890 US census. The 1880 census had taken more than 8 years, and the 1890 count was processed much faster. — [Wikipedia: Tabulating machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabulating_machine); [IBM: The punched card tabulator](https://ibm.com/history/punched-card-tabulator)
- **Company timeline:** Tabulating Machine Company (1896) → merged with others in 1911 to form the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company (CTR) → renamed IBM in 1924. — [Smithsonian NMAH: From Herman Hollerith to IBM](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object-groups/tabulating-equipment/from-herman-hollerith-to-ibm); [Wikipedia: CTR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing-Tabulating-Recording_Company)
- **Myth to avoid:** "Hollerith founded IBM". He founded a **predecessor** company. IBM was formed by merger and renaming under others, with Charles Flint arranging the 1911 merger and Thomas J. Watson Sr. renaming it in 1924 **[Flint/Watson roles UNVERIFIED this session]**.
- Patent dates conflict: some sources give 1884 (his first application), and Britannica says he patented a tabulating machine in 1889. — [Britannica Kids: Hollerith](https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/Herman-Hollerith/326493)
- *How it works (general knowledge, **[UNVERIFIED detail]**):* each person is one card with holes in set positions (age band, sex, and so on). In the card press, spring-loaded pins pass through the holes into cups of mercury, which closes a circuit. That moves a dial counter forward and can open a sorting-box lid.
- *Person:* Herman Hollerith 1860–1929 **[UNVERIFIED years]**. Died more than 70 years ago.
- **Challenge:** "Tabulate census cards": the player punches cards for 20 villagers, then runs them through the press. Counters tick up, and sorting-box lids pop open to answer "how many farmers over 40?". This is a hands-on lesson in records, fields and queries.

**14. Women "computers" by hand: Harvard College Observatory (c. 1880s–1920s)**
- Under director Edward C. Pickering, Williamina Fleming (first hired as Pickering's maid), Annie Jump Cannon, Henrietta Swan Leavitt and others reduced photographic plates and catalogued stars. Cannon catalogued thousands of stars by their spectral lines. — [FAS37: The Harvard Computers](https://www.fas37.org/wp/harvard-computers/); [Wikipedia: Henrietta Swan Leavitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt)
- Leavitt's permanent post (1902) paid about 30 cents an hour, or $10.50 a week. The claim that Pickering hired women to save money is reported, not documented. — [Wikipedia: Henrietta Swan Leavitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt); [Encyclopedia.com: Leavitt](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/science-and-technology/astronomy-biographies/henrietta-leavitt)
- Leavitt's 1912 circular, published under Pickering's name as director, used 25 Cepheid variables in the Small Magellanic Cloud to show that period and brightness are related (the Leavitt Law). This later became a "standard candle" for distances. — [APS News: Death of Henrietta Swan Leavitt](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2017/12/death-henrietta-swan-leavitt-1921); [Astrobites](https://astrobites.org/2019/03/08/leavitt-variable-stars/)
- *People:* Leavitt died 12 December 1921 ([APS](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2017/12/death-henrietta-swan-leavitt-1921)). Pickering 1846–1919, Fleming 1857–1911, Cannon 1863–1941 **[UNVERIFIED years]**. All died more than 70 years ago.
- **Challenge:** "Be a computer": sort 30 stellar spectra into O B A F G K M (Cannon's scheme, mnemonic "Oh Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me") against a timer. Or plot period against brightness for 10 Cepheids and spot the line (Leavitt), which is a hands-on lesson in data analysis and finding patterns.

**15. Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem (1928) → Gödel (1931) → Church/Turing (1936)**
- Hilbert and Ackermann posed the Entscheidungsproblem in 1928 (*Grundzüge der theoretischen Logik*): is there an effective procedure that decides whether any first-order statement is valid? Hilbert also posed it as one of three questions at the 1928 international congress (Bologna). — [ScienceDirect topic: Entscheidungsproblem](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/mathematics/entscheidungsproblem); [SEP: Church-Turing Thesis](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2018/entries/church-turing/)
- Gödel's 1931 paper "On Formally Undecidable Propositions of Principia Mathematica and Related Systems" showed that any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove. This dealt the first blow to Hilbert's programme, but it **left the Entscheidungsproblem open**. His 1930 completeness theorem did not settle it either. — [SEP: Computability and Complexity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/computability/); [Staatsinstitut pubpub](https://staatsinstitut.pubpub.org/pub/entscheidungsproblem/release/1). The journal (*Monatshefte für Mathematik und Physik*) is **[UNVERIFIED this session]**.
- Church (lambda calculus) and Turing (machines) showed independently in 1936 that no such general procedure exists. — [SEP: Church-Turing Thesis](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2018/entries/church-turing/); [Wikipedia: History of the Church–Turing thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Church%E2%80%93Turing_thesis)
- *People:* David Hilbert 1862–1943 (more than 70 years ago). **Wilhelm Ackermann 1896–1962 (NOT 70+)**. **Kurt Gödel 1906–1978 (NOT 70+; died 48 years ago)**. **Alonzo Church 1903–1995 (NOT 70+)**. Alan Turing 1912–1954 (just over 70 years ago as of June 2026). **[All years UNVERIFIED this session]**
- **Challenges:** "Liar's lock": a door labelled "This door cannot be proven open" teaches the self-reference behind Gödel's theorem. "Decide or die": the player gets a list of short programs and must say which ones halt. Some can be decided, and one is built to defeat any decider. This sets up the 1936 floor.

## What common myths should be avoided?

### Takeaway
The most common errors are: overstating what Babbage built, crediting Lovelace either with everything or with nothing, calling Hollerith "the founder of IBM", treating the Antikythera epoch date as its build date, and implying that Gödel answered the Entscheidungsproblem.

### Cited Findings
- **"Babbage built the Analytical Engine":** false. Only a trial piece was finished before he died in 1871. Henry Babbage later built part of the mill. — [Science Museum](https://www.sciencemuseum.org.uk/objects-and-stories/charles-babbages-difference-engines-and-science-museum); [ScienceDirect overview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/analytical-engine)
- **"Babbage built a working Difference Engine":** only a one-seventh demonstration portion (1832, about 2,000 parts) of Engine No. 1 was built. — [Science Museum](https://www.sciencemuseum.org.uk/objects-and-stories/charles-babbages-difference-engines-and-science-museum)
- **Difference Engine No. 2** was first built in 1991 by the Science Museum (calculating section), with the printer added in 2002. It proved the design would have worked. It is a modern build from Babbage's drawings, not a Victorian machine. — [Science Museum Group co62748](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co62748); [CHM: A Modern Sequel](https://www.computerhistory.org/babbage/modernsequel/)
- **"Lovelace wrote the first program alone" or "Lovelace contributed nothing":** both are oversimplified. The Notes are hers and are signed A.A.L. Babbage suggested the Bernoulli problem. The evidence points to collaboration, with documentary evidence that she wrote out the Note G table. — [Wikipedia: Note G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_G); [CACM blog](https://cacm.acm.org/blogcacm/was-ada-lovelace-actually-the-first-programmer/); [ResearchGate (Misa-cited)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366984308_Charles_Babbage_Ada_Lovelace_and_the_Bernoulli_Numbers)
- **"Lovelace's program ran":** it never ran, because there was no machine. — [Wikipedia: Note G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_G)
- **"Lovelace said machines can't be creative, so she was wrong":** "Lovelace's Objection" is Turing's 1950 name, taken by way of Hartree. Commentators say Turing took her words out of context. — [Turing 1950](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/activities/ieg/e-library/sources/t_article.pdf); [ResearchGate: Turing's responses to two objections](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/226952868_Turing's_Responses_to_Two_Objections)
- **"Hollerith founded IBM":** he founded the Tabulating Machine Company (1896). CTR was formed by merger in 1911, and IBM was the 1924 rename. — [Smithsonian NMAH](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object-groups/tabulating-equipment/from-herman-hollerith-to-ibm)
- **"Antikythera was built in 205 BC":** 205 BCE is the dial epoch. The build date is disputed and may be much later. — [Carman 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0021828617721553); [Jones 2020](http://dlib.nyu.edu/awdl/isaw/isaw-papers/17/)
- **"Gödel solved the Entscheidungsproblem":** no. Incompleteness left it open, and Church and Turing settled it in 1936. — [Staatsinstitut](https://staatsinstitut.pubpub.org/pub/entscheidungsproblem/release/1); [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/computability/)
- **"Jacquard invented punched cards":** Bouchon (1725) and Falcon (1728) came first. Jacquard combined their cards with Vaucanson's mechanism. — [Wikipedia: Jacquard machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacquard_machine)
- **"Pascal built the first calculator":** Schickard's 1623 design comes first, though whether it was finished is disputed. — [Wikipedia: Wilhelm Schickard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Schickard)
- **"Leibniz invented binary because of the I Ching":** this is disputed. He may have developed it first. — [Journal of East-West Thought](https://scholarworks.calstate.edu/downloads/xk81jr681)
- **"Leavitt's 1912 paper was Pickering's discovery":** his name appears as director, but the data and insight were Leavitt's. — [Wikipedia: Henrietta Swan Leavitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt)
- **"Oughtred invented the slide rule in 1622" stated without nuance:** there is no 1622 publication. The first printed descriptions are 1632/1633, and Delamain disputed priority. — [Cajori](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/42216/42216-h/42216-h.htm)

### Inferences
- Floor text should say "designed" for the Analytical Engine and "partly built" for Difference Engine No. 1. The 1991 machine can be shown as a "proof from the future".
- For Ada, the safest line is that she wrote the Notes, including the first published algorithm intended for a general-purpose machine, working with Babbage.

### Gaps
- I did not find a source on the claim that Babbage's engines failed because of "Victorian engineering limits". The 1991 build is usually cited as evidence that the precision was achievable, but I did not see this stated in a source this session.

## What is the accurate order of ideas, and where did ideas overlap in time?

### Takeaway
The order is: tally/abacus → (c. 2nd century BC) Antikythera → 1614–1633 logarithms, bones, slide rule, Schickard (overlapping) → 1642 Pascal → 1673 Leibniz → 1703 binary → 1725–1804 punched-card looms → 1822–1871 Babbage, with the 1840–1843 Lovelace/De Morgan/Menabrea overlap → 1847–1854 Boole/De Morgan → 1879 Frege → 1880s–1920s Hollerith *and* the Harvard computers (overlapping) → 1928 Hilbert → 1931 Gödel → 1936 Church/Turing.

### Cited Findings
- Napier's logs (1614) and bones (1617), Schickard's letters using bones (1623–1624), and Oughtred's rule (c. 1622 invented, printed 1632) all fall within about 20 years of each other. — [NMS](https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/napiers-bones-the-logarithmic-genius-who-revolutionised-calculations); [Wikipedia: Schickard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Schickard); [Cajori](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/42216/42216-h/42216-h.htm)
- The Babbage engines overlap with Lovelace's De Morgan tutoring (1840–1841) and the Menabrea translation (1843). De Morgan and Boole both published in 1847, while Babbage was still designing. Babbage died in 1871, the same year as De Morgan **[De Morgan death year UNVERIFIED]**. — [CHM history](https://www.computerhistory.org/babbage/history/); [Hollings et al. 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0315086017300319); [SEP: Algebra of Logic](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/algebra-logic-tradition/)
- Hollerith's tabulation (1890) and the Harvard computers (from the 1880s, Leavitt 1912) run in parallel. Industrial punched-card tabulation and human hand computation happened at the same time. — [IBM](https://ibm.com/history/punched-card-tabulator); [FAS37](https://www.fas37.org/wp/harvard-computers/)
- Frege's work (1879–1903) ran in parallel with the algebraic tradition (Peirce, Schröder) and had little early influence. — [SEP: Emergence of First-Order Logic](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/logic-firstorder-emergence/)
- 1928 → 1931 → 1936 is a clear chain of question, partial blow, and answer. — [SEP: Church-Turing Thesis](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2018/entries/church-turing/)

### Inferences
- Suggested floor order (one floor per moment): (1) Abacus → (2) Antikythera → (3) Napier's bones/logs → (4) Slide rule → (5) Schickard → (6) Pascaline → (7) Leibniz reckoner + binary → (8) Jacquard loom → (9) Difference Engine → (10) Analytical Engine + Lovelace/Menabrea Notes → (11) Boole & De Morgan → (12) Frege → (13) Harvard computers → (14) Hollerith census → (15) Hilbert's question → Boss Floor: Gödel 1931 / Turing 1936. Schickard can be merged with Napier, and Frege with Boole, to save floors.
- **Depicting real people:** Gödel, Church and Ackermann died fewer than 70 years ago (Gödel 1978, Church 1995, Ackermann 1962). Turing (died 1954) has only just passed 70 years, and Watson Sr. (1956) is borderline. Everyone else on the list died more than 70 years ago. Living people tied to these floors (Swade, Myhrvold, Freeth) should not be shown as characters. This is design caution, not legal advice. Years are UNVERIFIED and should be confirmed.
