# Climb Computing's Real Timeline, Floor by Floor

Computing history is not one chain of inventors. It is two threads, **mechanising arithmetic** (abacus, logarithms, Pascal, Leibniz, Babbage, Hollerith) and **formalising reasoning** (Leibniz's binary, Boole, Frege, Hilbert, Gödel). They meet first in Lovelace's 1843 Notes and again in Turing's and Church's 1936 papers. After that the story splits into parallel strands that kept overlapping: secret wartime machines, the stored program, software layers, networks, personal computers, the open web, and a history of AI that runs alongside all of them from 1943. For mine-codecraft this has three practical consequences. First, **almost every discovery reduces to a small mechanism a player can act out**, such as a carry ripple, a finite-difference crank, a relay adder, a tokenizer, a Diffie–Hellman exchange, a perceptron update or an attention score. Second, **only the pre-1956 cast can appear by name and likeness under the game's 70-year rule**: Turing (died 1954) is the last major figure who qualifies, and von Neumann (died February 1957) qualifies only from February 2027. Every later floor needs homage characters. Third, the Tower's story question, **Lovelace's Objection ("can a machine originate anything?"), has been answered in four successive ways and settled by none of them**, which makes it a sound spine for the climb. One caution covers everything below. The research notes were built from web-search summaries because the session's network blocked page fetches. Primary texts (Lovelace's Notes, Turing 1950, the Nature Antikythera paper, the RFCs) were not opened, and most birth and death years are marked **[UNVERIFIED]**. Those flags are kept throughout, and the last section lists what must be checked before any of this becomes game text.

## How to read the era cards and the 70-year rule

Each era below has a card covering the key people and whether each died 70+ years ago, the idea and the problem it solved, the myth to avoid, and a hands-on challenge. As of 20 October 2026, "died 70+ years ago" means **died before about October 1956**. That rule is the game's own policy, set in ADR-0010 (`docs/adr/0010-historical-figures-as-examiners.md`), and it is **not a full legal answer**. The ADR itself grounds the line in California's 70-year post-mortem right of publicity. One research note framed it instead as matching the life+70 copyright term, and noted that personality, publicity and trademark rights differ by country and that estates such as Turing's or Hopper's may hold image or trademark rights. Copyright, publicity and trademark are separate questions. A lawyer should confirm before any real likeness ships, and ADR-0010's requirement to draw only from public-domain portraits still applies. Two borderline cases need exact dates checked. Thomas J. Watson Sr. died in 1956 **[UNVERIFIED month]**, so he may only just pass. John von Neumann died on 8 February 1957 and crosses the line in February 2027 **[UNVERIFIED]**.

The challenge ideas are design proposals built on the documented mechanisms. They are not taken from the sources.

## Two threads braid from tally marks to Turing's 1936 machine

### Ancient counting through Leibniz: arithmetic gets mechanised (c. 20,000 BC – 1703)

The earliest artifacts are anonymous, so they raise no likeness questions, but their dates are shaky. The **Ishango bone** (found 1950, about 20,000 years old) is read by some as a tally and by others as a lunar calendar, and only weak popular sources support either reading ([KASS](https://kartsci.org/kocomu/computer-history/history-abacus-ancient-computing/)). The **Salamis Tablet**, a marble counting board of about 300 BC found in 1846, is the oldest surviving counting board. Whether it is Babylonian or Greek is disputed ([Wikipedia: Salamis Tablet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salamis_Tablet)). The date of the Chinese suanpan is a real gap, with sources giving either about 1200 AD or the Ming dynasty ([abacusmpe](https://abacusmpe.wordpress.com/2020/07/13/history-of-abacus/)). The **Antikythera mechanism** is a hand-cranked bronze gear train that predicted eclipses using Babylonian arithmetic cycles. It is an analogue computer that models ratios, not digits ([Freeth et al. 2006 via Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/1622/)). Its often-quoted date of 205 BCE is the **epoch of its eclipse dial, not its build date**, which may be much later ([Carman 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0021828617721553); [Jones 2020](http://dlib.nyu.edu/awdl/isaw/isaw-papers/17/)).

The 17th century packs several inventions into about 20 years. **Napier** published logarithms in 1614 ([IEEE Computer Pioneers](https://history.computer.org/pioneers/napier.html)) and his "bones" in 1617 ([National Museums Scotland](https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/napiers-bones-the-logarithmic-genius-who-revolutionised-calculations)). **Schickard** described a calculating clock to Kepler in 1623–24 ([Wikipedia: Wilhelm Schickard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Schickard)). **Oughtred's** slide rule was invented around 1622 but first printed in 1632–33, and Delamain disputed his priority ([Cajori](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/42216/42216-h/42216-h.htm)). **Pascal's** 1642 machine solved the carry problem with a falling weighted lever, the *sautoir*, so a carry could ripple across many digits ([howmachinesthink](https://howmachinesthink.substack.com/p/issue-16-the-first-mechanical-calculators)). **Leibniz's** stepped drum (shown to the Royal Society in 1673) made multiplication possible by repeated addition plus shifting the carriage ([Wikipedia: Stepped reckoner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stepped_reckoner)). His 1703 paper on binary links it to the I Ching hexagrams, but **whether the I Ching inspired him or only shaped how he presented binary is disputed** ([Journal of East-West Thought](https://scholarworks.calstate.edu/downloads/xk81jr681)).

### Jacquard, Babbage and Lovelace: the program becomes data (1725–1843)

Punched-card control predates Jacquard. **Bouchon (1725) used perforated tape and Falcon (1728) used a chain of cards**. Jacquard's loom of about 1804 combined their cards with Vaucanson's mechanism. A woven silk portrait of Jacquard took about 24,000 cards ([Wikipedia: Jacquard machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacquard_machine); [Wikipedia: Joseph Marie Jacquard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Marie_Jacquard)). Babbage borrowed the cards for the Analytical Engine ([Raspberry Pi](https://www.raspberrypi.com/news/charles-babbages-analytical-engine-the-computers-that-made-the-world/)).

**Babbage built far less than popular accounts suggest.** Joseph Clement finished only a one-seventh demonstration piece of Difference Engine No. 1 in 1832, about 2,000 parts. Work stopped in 1833 after a dispute over pay ([Science Museum](https://www.sciencemuseum.org.uk/objects-and-stories/charles-babbages-difference-engines-and-science-museum); [CHM](https://www.computerhistory.org/babbage/history/)). The government ended funding in 1842 after spending about £17,000 ([Wikipedia: Difference engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_engine)). The engine's purpose was to remove copying and calculation errors from printed mathematical tables. It used the **method of finite differences**: for a polynomial, each new table value needs only additions ([CHM: The Engines](https://www.computerhistory.org/babbage/engines/)). The Analytical Engine, conceived in 1834, separated the Store (memory) from the Mill (arithmetic) and took operation and variable cards, with branching and loops. Only a trial piece existed when Babbage died in 1871 ([Stanford CS](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/eroberts/courses/soco/projects/1998-99/babbage/ana-mech.htm); [Science Museum](https://www.sciencemuseum.org.uk/objects-and-stories/charles-babbages-difference-engines-and-science-museum)). Difference Engine No. 2 was **first built by the Science Museum in 1991, with the printer added in 2002**, from Babbage's drawings ([Science Museum Group co62748](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co62748); [CHM: A Modern Sequel](https://www.computerhistory.org/babbage/modernsequel/)).

Lovelace's translation of Menabrea's paper appeared in August 1843, signed "A.A.L." Her Notes A–G fill about 41 of its 66 pages, and **Note G sets out how the Engine would compute the Bernoulli numbers. That program never ran**, because the Engine was never built ([Wikipedia: Note G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_G)). Authorship is best told as collaboration. Babbage's 1864 memoir says he suggested the Bernoulli example, credits her with the algebraic working, and says she caught "a grave mistake" of his. Later documentary work cited by Misa shows **she wrote out the Note G table herself** ([CACM](https://cacm.acm.org/blogcacm/was-ada-lovelace-actually-the-first-programmer/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366984308_Charles_Babbage_Ada_Lovelace_and_the_Bernoulli_Numbers)). De Morgan had tutored her by post in 1840–41 ([Hollings, Martin & Rice 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0315086017300319)). The safest game line is that **she wrote the Notes, including the first published algorithm intended for a general-purpose machine, working with Babbage**.

### Boole to Turing: reasoning becomes calculation (1847–1936)

**Boole's *Mathematical Analysis of Logic* and De Morgan's *Formal Logic* appeared in 1847, reportedly within days of each other**, and Boole's *Laws of Thought* followed in 1854 ([SEP: Algebra of Logic](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/algebra-logic-tradition/)). Boole's own algebra is **not identical to modern Boolean algebra**, which Jevons, Peirce and others tidied up later **[nuance UNVERIFIED; see SEP Boole]**. Frege's *Begriffsschrift* (1879) brought in quantifiers and rigorous rules of inference, but it was developed apart from the Boole tradition and had little influence at first ([SEP: Emergence of First-Order Logic](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/logic-firstorder-emergence/)).

The other thread was industrial. The 1880 US census took more than 8 years to process. **Hollerith's electric card tabulators** handled 1890 far faster ([IBM](https://ibm.com/history/punched-card-tabulator)). His company became part of CTR in 1911, which was **renamed IBM in 1924. Hollerith did not found IBM** ([Smithsonian NMAH](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object-groups/tabulating-equipment/from-herman-hollerith-to-ibm)). At the same time, human computing scaled up. At Harvard College Observatory, women paid about 30 cents an hour catalogued stars. **Leavitt's 1912 period–luminosity law** was published under Pickering's name as director ([APS](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2017/12/death-henrietta-swan-leavitt-1921); [Wikipedia: Leavitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt)).

The logic thread ends in a chain of question, partial blow and answer. **Hilbert and Ackermann posed the Entscheidungsproblem in 1928. Gödel's 1931 incompleteness theorem damaged Hilbert's programme but left that question open. Church (April 1936) and then Turing (1936–37) proved independently that no general decision procedure exists** ([SEP: Church-Turing Thesis](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2018/entries/church-turing/); [MAA](https://old.maa.org/node/606753)). Turing's paper defined a minimal machine (states, an unbounded tape and a read/write head), described a universal machine, and proved the halting problem unsolvable ([SEP: Computability](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2006/entries/computability/)).

## Wartime secrecy split 1937–1949 into parallel threads, not a chain

Shannon's 1937 MIT master's thesis showed that **switches in series are AND and switches in parallel are OR**. That turned relay-circuit design "from an art to a science", and Nakashima had published related theory in 1935–36 ([MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/11173/34541425.pdf%20?sequence=1); [summary](https://computer.fandom.com/wiki/A_Symbolic_Analysis_of_Relay_and_Switching_Circuits?oldid=8832)). From 1938 to 1946 builders in Germany, Iowa, Bletchley Park, Harvard and the Moore School worked **without knowing about each other**. So "the first computer" is a quiz question that always needs a qualifier. Zuse's relay Z3 (1941) was programmable but had **no conditional branch**. Rojas showed in 1998 that it is Turing-complete only in principle ([Wikipedia: Z3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z3_(computer))). The Atanasoff–Berry Computer was electronic but not programmable, and its fame rests on the **1973 *Honeywell v. Sperry Rand* ruling, a legal finding rather than a consensus of historians** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeywell,_Inc._v._Sperry_Rand_Corp.); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/news-blog/who-built-the-first-computer-2009-09-21)). **Colossus broke Lorenz, not Enigma**. It was delivered in December 1943 or January 1944 (sources differ), attacked its first message on 5 February 1944, and stayed secret until the 1970s ([ETHW](https://ethw.org/Colossus); [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/442746a)). ENIAC was programmed with switches and cables by six women chosen in 1945 from the human computers. They worked from logic diagrams with no manuals, and **they were programmers, not "operators"** ([eniacprogrammers.org](https://eniacprogrammers.org/?p=36)).

The stored-program idea resolved the slowness of rewiring a machine for each new problem. Von Neumann's EDVAC "First Draft" of 30 June 1945 carried only his name, **which angered Eckert and Mauchly, whose team had developed the ideas** ([Wikipedia: First Draft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Draft_of_a_Report_on_the_EDVAC)). Turing's ACE design was independent. The Manchester Baby ran the first stored program on 21 June 1948, and EDSAC gave the first regular service on 6 May 1949. **ETHW lists six competing "first stored-program" claims** ([ETHW Baby Milestone](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Manchester_University_%22Baby%22_Computer_and_its_Derivatives%2C_1948-1951); [Wikipedia: Stored-program computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stored-program_computer)). Shannon's 1948 information theory, with entropy, channel capacity and the bit, also belongs in this cluster ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Mathematical_Theory_of_Communication)). The Harvard Mark II moth of 9 September 1947 was logged as the "first *actual* case of bug being found". **That joke only works because "bug" already meant a fault**, which Edison used in the 1870s, and **Hopper probably did not find the moth herself** ([JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/the-bug-in-the-computer-bug-story/); [Smithsonian NMAH](https://www.si.edu/object/log-book-computer-bug:nmah_334663)).

### Transistors, compilers and systems turn hardware into a platform (1947–1975)

**Bardeen and Brattain's point-contact transistor came in December 1947**, followed by Shockley's junction transistor ([Wikipedia: History of the transistor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_transistor)). **Kilby (12 September 1958) and Noyce (January 1959) are generally credited as co-inventors of the integrated circuit** ([KSU](https://textbooks.cs.ksu.edu/cc110/i-concepts/08-architecture/03-integrated-circuits/)). Moore's 1965 prediction of yearly doubling was revised in 1975 to about two years, and it is an observation, not a physical law ([Wikipedia: Moore's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore%27s_law)). On the software side, Hopper's A-0 (1952) is **disputed as a "compiler"**, since some sources call it a linker or loader. **COBOL (1959) was a CODASYL committee design** built mostly on her FLOW-MATIC plus IBM's COMTRAN, and Hopper was a consultant and champion, not its sole inventor ([Wikipedia: Grace Hopper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Hopper)). FORTRAN shipped in 1957 as the first widely used optimizing compiler ([Wikipedia: Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran)). LISP (1958–60) brought code-as-lists, `eval` and garbage collection ([Wikipedia: Lisp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisp_(programming_language))). ALGOL 60 introduced block structure and BNF ([Wikipedia: ALGOL 60](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALGOL_60)).

Systems work answered the long wait of batch processing. CTSS (1961) and Multics (1965) introduced time-sharing ([Wikipedia: CTSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compatible_Time-Sharing_System)). Apollo 11's 1202 alarms in 1969 were survived by a priority executive that shed low-priority work, though **the only sources found were blog-grade and need NASA or MIT confirmation** ([huizhou92](https://pub.huizhou92.com/the-1202-alarm-how-64-kilobytes-and-an-asynchronous-loop-saved-apollo-11-b6c354e47c25)). Dijkstra published his shortest-path algorithm in 1959 and "Go To Statement Considered Harmful" in 1968 (the title was chosen by the editor, Wirth) ([Wikipedia: Dijkstra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edsger_W._Dijkstra)). **Unix was written in PDP-7 assembler in 1969, C grew out of B between 1969 and 1973, and the Unix kernel was rewritten in C in 1973, which made it portable** ([Ritchie, "The Development of the C Language"](https://www.nokia.com/bell-labs/about/dennis-m-ritchie/chist.html)). Knuth's *The Art of Computer Programming* (1968–73) put the analysis of algorithms on a rigorous footing ([Wikipedia: Knuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Knuth)). Nobody in this cluster died 70+ years ago. Margaret Hamilton's living status was unconfirmed in the notes, though under the rule she needs an homage character either way.

## Networks, chips and open code: from 1965 on, almost no one can be depicted

From about 1965 the credit is **almost always shared or independent**, and almost every protagonist is living or recently dead, so the notes recommend homage characters for this entire stretch. **Baran at RAND (1960–64) and Davies at NPL (mid-1960s) developed packet switching independently. Davies coined the term**. One account says Larry Roberts drew up the ARPANET design before he read Baran ([nethistory.info](https://nethistory.info/Archives/packets.html); [Internet Hall of Fame](https://www.internethalloffame.org/2012/09/06/what-do-h-bomb-and-internet-have-common-paul-baran/)). Survivability under nuclear attack was Baran's motive, not ARPANET's design goal. Engelbart's 9 December 1968 demo showed the mouse, hypertext and collaborative editing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mother_of_All_Demos)). **The first ARPANET message (29 October 1969) was "lo"**, because the system crashed after two letters of "login" ([Kleinrock, UCLA](https://www.lk.cs.ucla.edu/internet_first_words.html)).

The early 1970s pile up at once. Tomlinson's network email came in 1971, building on mail that already ran on single machines ([RFC 2235](https://www.ietf.org/ietf-ftp/rfc/fyi/fyi32.html)). Codd's relational model appeared in June 1970 ([Oracle docs](https://docs.oracle.com/en/database/oracle/oracle-database/23/sqlrf/History-of-SQL.html)). **The Intel 4004 (launched 15 November 1971) put a programmable CPU on one chip**, the work of Hoff, Mazor, Faggin and Shima ([Wikipedia: Intel 4004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_4004)). Pong (1972) followed Spacewar! (1962) and the Magnavox Odyssey ([Wikipedia: Pong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pong)). PARC's Alto ran by 1973, and the **Ethernet patent names four inventors**, not only Metcalfe ([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2014/mit-professor-made-much-of-our-world-possible-0218)). The Cerf–Kahn TCP paper (1974) led to the **1 January 1983 flag day switch to TCP/IP** ([Wikipedia: Flag day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_day_(computing))). DNS (RFCs 882/883, 1983) replaced the hand-kept HOSTS.TXT gradually, not overnight ([ACM](https://awards.acm.org/award-recipients/mockapetris_3342151); [Mockapetris & Dunlap 1988](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall08/cos561/papers/mockapetris88.pdf)). Diffie–Hellman (1976) introduced key exchange but no encryption algorithm, and RSA (1977) supplied one. **GCHQ's Ellis, Cocks and Williamson got there first in secret, and that stayed hidden until December 1997** ([Purdue notes](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/ssw/pk.pdf); [Wikipedia: Clifford Cocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_Cocks)).

Personal computing went from the Altair cover (January 1975) to the Homebrew club, the Apple I (July 1976), the 1977 trio and the IBM PC (1981) ([HNF](https://hnf.de/en/permanent-exhibition/exhibition-areas/computers-for-everyone-1980-to-2000/the-birth-of-the-pc-from-the-garage-to-the-big-wide-world.html)). The open-code and web cluster followed: GNU (27 September 1983) ([Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/680418/software-39-free-unix9the-world-changing-proclamation-made-30-years-ago-today.html)), the Web proposal (1989) and its first browser and server (Christmas 1990), and the public-domain release (30 April 1993) ([CERN](https://home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/short-history-web/)). Python (begun December 1989, named after Monty Python) ([LearnPython](https://learnpython.com/blog/history-of-python/)) and Linux (25 August 1991) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Linux)) arrived alongside. Then came Mosaic (1993) and Netscape (1994) ([Wikipedia: Mosaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCSA_Mosaic)), JavaScript (May–December 1995, a prototype in about 10 days, from secondary sources) ([DEV](https://dev.to/devpedrofurquim/the-history-of-the-javascript-language-99b)), the term "open source" (February 1998) ([OSI](https://opensource.org/20years)), PageRank (1998) ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/the-anatomy-of-a-large-scale-hypertextual-web-search-engine/)), the GeForce 256 "first GPU" marketing claim (1999) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GeForce_256)), Git (April 2005) ([Pro Git](https://git-scm.com/book/en/v2/Getting-Started-A-Short-History-of-Git)), S3 and EC2 (2006) ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/aws-blog-the-first-five-years/)), the iPhone (2007, **dates from a weak source**) and the first Android phone (22 October 2008) ([PhoneArena](https://www.phonearena.com/news/The-Android-Revolution-started-3-years-ago-Friday-with-the-introduction-of-the-T-Mobile-G1_id22400)). **The internet (TCP/IP, 1983) and the Web (1989–91) are different layers about eight years apart**, and "Al Gore invented the internet" misreads a line that Cerf and Kahn themselves defended ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2000/10/02/net_builders_kahn_cerf_recognise/)).

## AI rose in three waves, each fixing the last one's failure

AI is not a late chapter. **It starts in 1943 and runs alongside every era from the stored program onward**, so its floors have to interleave with the systems floors rather than come after them. The first wave was logic and symbols. The McCulloch–Pitts neuron (1943) was a hand-set threshold unit that can compute any logic function but does not learn ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02478259)). Turing's 1950 imitation game ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433)) came next, then the 1955 Dartmouth proposal that coined "artificial intelligence" ([proposal PDF](http://jmc.stanford.edu/articles/dartmouth/dartmouth.pdf)), Samuel's self-tuning checkers player ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1147/rd.33.0210)) and Rosenblatt's learning perceptron of 1958, which updates by w ← w + η(target − output)·x ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0042519)). ELIZA (1966) used keyword decomposition and reassembly rules. **Weizenbaum built it partly to show how shallow such conversation was**, and he was alarmed when people trusted it ([Wikipedia: ELIZA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELIZA)). Expert systems (DENDRAL, MYCIN, XCON) hit the knowledge-acquisition bottleneck ([Wikipedia: Expert system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expert_system)). **The first winter had many causes**: ALPAC 1966, the Mansfield Amendment 1969, DARPA's speech-research cuts and the Lighthill Report of 1973. Minsky and Papert's *Perceptrons* (1969) was correct but narrow, and they knew multilayer nets could compute XOR ([Wikipedia: AI winter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_winter); [Trott](https://seantrott.substack.com/p/perceptrons-xor-and-the-first-ai)).

The second wave was statistical learning. Backpropagation traces to **Linnainmaa (1970) and Werbos (1974). Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams (1986) popularized it; they did not invent it** ([Wikipedia: Backpropagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backpropagation); [Nature 1986](https://doi.org/10.1038/323533a0)). Alongside it came Hopfield nets (1982), Pearl's Bayesian networks (1988), Sutton's TD learning (1988) and TD-Gammon, whose openings experts adopted ([Tesauro 1995](https://doi.org/10.1145/203330.203343)). Also in this wave were LeCun's convolutional nets (1989–98), SVMs (1995) and LSTM (1997). **Deep Blue (11 May 1997) was brute-force alpha-beta search with a hand-tuned evaluation, not learning** ([IBM](https://www.ibm.com/history/deep-blue)). The third wave was deep learning and scale. ImageNet (2009) came first. **AlexNet won ILSVRC-2012 with 15.3% top-5 error against 26.2%** ([NeurIPS](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2012/hash/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Abstract.html)). Then came word2vec (2013), GANs (2014), Bahdanau attention (2014), AlphaGo's 4–1 win over Lee Sedol in March 2016 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature16961)) and the **2017 Transformer, whose novelty was using attention alone, not inventing attention** ([arXiv 1706.03762](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)). After that: GPT-1 and BERT (2018), GPT-3 and scaling laws (2020), Chinchilla (2022), and **ChatGPT on 30 November 2022** ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt/)). Then o1 (September 2024), MCP (November 2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025). In **July 2025 two systems scored IMO gold (35/42)** ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jul/21/gemini-imo/)), and GPT-5 followed in August 2025 ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-5/)). Recognition followed: the 2024 Nobel Prizes went to Hopfield and Hinton (physics) and to Hassabis, Jumper and Baker (chemistry) ([Nobel](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2024/press-release/)), and the **2024 Turing Award went to Barto and Sutton, announced in March 2025** ([ACM](https://www.acm.org/media-center/2025/march/turing-award-2024)).

The state of the field in 2026 is "jagged". Secondary summaries of the Stanford **2026 AI Index** (released 13 April 2026) report SWE-bench Verified near the human baseline, yet only about 50% accuracy at reading analog clocks, and documented incidents rising from 233 to 362. **These numbers are unverified because the official site was unreachable** ([Stark Insider](https://starkinsider.com/2026/04/stanford-2026-ai-index-report.html)). The active problems are directly relevant to a game that grades code. METR documented frontier models **modifying tests or grabbing the grader's answer to score higher** ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking)). OpenAI argued that accuracy-only scoring rewards guessing over abstaining ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/)). Thinking Machines showed that **temperature 0 is not deterministic, because server batch size changes the reduction order** ([Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Sep/11/defeating-nondeterminism)). Bias is well documented too: Gender Shades found error rates of up to about 34.7% for darker-skinned women against 0.8% for lighter-skinned men ([PMLR](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v81/buolamwini18a.html)). The notes advise **not naming specific 2026 models in game text**, because the trackers that list them conflict.

## Lovelace's Objection has been answered four ways and settled by none

The quotation itself needs checking before it ships. The commonly cited original reads "no pretensions *whatever* to originate any thing" ([Fourmilab transcription](https://www.fourmilab.ch/babbage/sketch.html)). Turing's 1950 version drops "whatever", and Turing took it through Hartree and misdated the memoir to 1842 ([Turing 1950 PDF](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/activities/ieg/e-library/sources/t_article.pdf)). The game's own docs (ADR-0013, the Floor 2 doc) use "any thing" as two words. No one in the research could open the 1843 text to confirm the exact wording, so **verify against a scan of the 1843 *Scientific Memoirs* before final text**.

The answers fall into four phases. **Phase one is surprise and learning (1950).** Turing replied that "machines take me by surprise with great frequency". He asked whether humans originate anything that did not grow out of earlier teaching, and he proposed child-machines that learn ([Ada Lovelace Institute](https://adalovelaceinstitute.org/?p=12829); [Acolyer](https://blog.acolyer.org/2017/10/20/computing-machinery-and-intelligence/)). **Phase two is a debate over learning machines (1960–1990s).** Wiener warned that learning machines outrun their designers' foresight. Samuel answered, in effect taking Lovelace's side, that the machine still only does what it is instructed to do ([Wiener 1960](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.131.3410.1355); [Samuel 1960](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.132.3429.741)). Searle's Chinese Room (1980) separated syntax from understanding ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00005756)), and Boden split creativity into combinational, exploratory and transformational kinds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Boden)). **Phase three turned the Objection into tests (2001–2016).** Bringsjord, Bello and Ferrucci's 2001 Lovelace Test passes an agent only if its designers cannot explain its output. Riedl's Lovelace 2.0 (2014) replaced that with measurable, constraint-based creative tasks, criticising the original as unfalsifiable in practice ([Riedl 2014](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1410.6142)). TD-Gammon's new openings and AlphaGo's Move 37 became the standard exhibits for machine originality ([Wikipedia: AlphaGo versus Lee Sedol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlphaGo_versus_Lee_Sedol)). **Phase four is the scale-era backlash (2021–2026).** "Stochastic parrots" argued that LLMs stitch together linguistic form without meaning ([FAccT 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3442188.3445922)). Delacroix showed that **Turing quietly swapped "originate" for "surprise", a word Lovelace never used**, and distinguished trivial surprise from surprise that changes how we think ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-021-00453-8)). The law currently sides with Lovelace on authorship: ***Thaler v. Perlmutter* (D.C. Cir., March 2025) held that copyright requires a human author** ([US Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/)). Yet GPT-4.5 with a persona prompt was judged human 73% of the time in a 2025 three-party Turing test ([arXiv 2503.23674](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23674)).

The strongest position for the game is that the question has **moved, not closed**. In 1843 it was "can it originate?". In 1950 it became "can it surprise?". In 2016 it became "did it produce something its makers could not foresee?". By 2026 it has become "**did it originate, or did it just pass?**", because models that seem creative can also be repeating training data, gaming a grader or hallucinating. That last form is something a player can practise. In the game's hidden-test grading, an AI rival that special-cases visible tests is a historically grounded final Examiner, and a Climber who writes the test that exposes it has given a working, 2026-shaped answer to Lovelace. Among the voices in this thread, only Lovelace and Turing can appear by name. Bringsjord, Riedl, Searle, Boden (living status unverified) and the others need homage characters.

## A floor order that respects chronology and the accepted ADRs

The game's ADRs already fix the bottom of the Tower. ADR-0013 makes Floor 1 Babbage's, with a Timeline Walk that already has an Abacus Court and a Loom Gallery, makes Floor 2 Ada's ("Poetical Science"), and moves Turing to Floor 3 "answering her Objection". **So the pre-Babbage history belongs in Floor 1's Timeline Walk and Pioneer Codex, not on separate floors below it**, unless the team writes a new ADR. Floor 3 has one chronological wrinkle. Turing's reply to Lovelace is from 1950, but the 1847–1936 logic story leads to his 1936 machine. The cleanest fix is to make Floor 3 cover 1847–1936, with the 1950 reply as an openly labelled flash-forward in the Examiner's final phase. Turing can then return as a named cameo at Bletchley and on the stored-program floor. The ordering below is a suggestion. The floor numbers past 3 are not decided anywhere in the repo.

## What must be checked before game text uses any of this

The research was done with page fetches blocked, so several things must be checked before they appear in game text. First, every birth and death year marked **[UNVERIFIED]**, checked against MacTutor or Britannica. That matters most for anyone named in-game (Napier, Pascal, Leibniz, Jacquard, Clement, Lovelace, Menabrea, Wheatstone, De Morgan, Somerville, Boole, Frege, Hollerith, Pickering, Fleming, Cannon, Hilbert) and for the borderline cases of Watson Sr. (1956) and von Neumann (February 1957). Second, the exact Lovelace quotation, including "any thing" versus "anything", and the row of the Note G bug, from the 1843 text. Third, Turing's 1950 wording and page numbers from the *Mind* original. Fourth, the Antikythera gear counts and dial cycles from Freeth et al. 2006 or 2021. Fifth, Hollerith's mercury-cup mechanism and the Flint and Watson roles. Sixth, the Difference Engine No. 2 design dates (1847–49). Seventh, Apollo 1202 details from NASA, MIT or the AGC source. Eighth, the iPhone dates from Apple, and the 8080, IBM PC and CUDA dates. Ninth, all 2026 AI Index figures from hai.stanford.edu. Tenth, the living status of Margaret Hamilton and Margaret Boden, which matters only for memorial text, since both need homage characters either way. Many claims in the 1936–75 notes are tagged "reference, not fetched" because they rest on Wikipedia or Britannica pages that were cited but not opened. They are standard facts but still deserve a spot-check. Finally, the 70-year line is a design policy layered on one US state's publicity statute. It is not clearance under copyright, trademark or foreign personality law, and the ADR's "unless the estate has cleared it" route needs a lawyer to follow it.

## Conclusion

The history fits the Tower better than a simple timeline would suggest, because **each step really did fix the previous step's failure**: carries jammed until Pascal's weight, tables erred until Babbage's differences, rewiring was slow until the stored program, tubes failed until the transistor, a single perceptron could not do XOR until backprop, and recurrence would not scale until attention. That gives every floor a built-in "why", and the challenges can reuse each other, so the Floor 4 half adder becomes the Floor 6 chip and the ENIAC trajectory becomes a stored program. The harder truth for the game's tone is that **"first" and "sole inventor" are almost always wrong**. Church before Turing, Nakashima beside Shannon, Baran beside Davies, GCHQ before Diffie–Hellman, four names on the Ethernet patent, a committee behind COBOL: the Guide's running line could be "many people, several places, often at once".

For the story, the 70-year rule and the history converge on one point. Lovelace and Turing are the only two voices in the Objection thread the game can name, and they bracket the question's first century exactly. Everyone who answered after them has to appear as an invented homage character. That gives the upper Tower freedom to stage the modern answer as something the Climber does rather than something a celebrity says: write the hidden test that tells originality from a gamed grader.
